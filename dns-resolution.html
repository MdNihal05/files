How a name becomes an address

the complete path of one DNS lookup — every cache, every referral, every packet — built from first principles and measured live on this machine, not recited. the previous version of this post annotated one dig +trace and called it an explanation; this one earns the trace by the time it appears.

The file that used to be the internet

start from the actual problem. programs open connections to addresses; humans and configs hold names. something has to map one to the other, for every machine on the network, and stay correct while machines appear, move and die.

the first solution was exactly what you'd build in an afternoon: a file. HOSTS.TXT, maintained by hand at the SRI Network Information Center; every host on the ARPANET periodically fetched the whole thing. it worked, and then it stopped working, and the RFC that introduced DNS says why in one sentence — quoted from RFC 882, november 1983:

"The size of this table, and especially the frequency of updates to the table are near the limit of manageability."

the failure is worth decomposing, because each piece becomes a design requirement:

  • one editor for the whole world's writes. adding your host means mailing the NIC and waiting. requirement: whoever owns a name should be able to change its records without asking anyone upstream — ownership has to be delegated.
  • every reader downloads everything. full-table sync scales as hosts × hosts. requirement: a lookup should fetch only what it asks about, and repeated lookups should cost nothing — reads have to be cached.
  • one distribution point. the NIC down means no updates for anyone. requirement: no single machine may sit on the hot path of every lookup.

DNS is the data structure those three requirements force: a tree of names with delegated ownership, queried piecewise, cached aggressively. everything below — referrals, glue, TTLs, even the DNSSEC design — falls out of that shape.

A tree, read right to left

mdnihal.online. is not a string — it's a path. the trailing dot (real, usually invisible) is the root of a tree; online is a child of the root; mdnihal a child of online. and the tree is cut into zones: contiguous chunks that one organization actually serves. the root zone is run by the twelve root server operators; the .online zone by the Radix registry; everything at mdnihal.online and below by my DNS host. a zone hands a subtree to the next owner with an NS record — that record is the delegation, and it's the only coupling between the layers.

delegation is the only thing crossing a zone boundary. the root does not know mdnihal.online exists — it knows who runs .online, full stop. record contents and TTLs measured today with dig +norecurse against a.root-servers.net and ns01.trs-dns.net.

4 named nodes · 4 delegation edges · 3 zones · root→online: NS ×4 + 8 glue records @ 172800s · online→mdnihal: NS ×2, 0 glue @ 900s

two details in that picture do real work later. the root's referral ships glue — the A/AAAA addresses of .online's servers ride along in the ADDITIONAL section, because otherwise you'd need DNS to bootstrap DNS. the TLD's referral ships no glue: dns-parking.com is not inside .online, so the registry has no authority over its address, and a resolver has to go resolve ns2.dns-parking.com as a separate lookup. both verified on the wire today — the root's referral carried 8 glue records, the TLD's carried none (ADDITIONAL held only the EDNS OPT record).

Three programs, three jobs

"the resolver" is three different programs, and knowing which one you're talking to is most of DNS debugging:

rolewhat it doeswhere it runs
stub resolverholds a small cache, asks one configured recursive resolver, waits. never walks the tree.your OS — here systemd-resolved on 127.0.0.53
recursive resolverdoes the actual walking: root → TLD → authoritative, following referrals. owns the cache that matters.your router, ISP, or 1.1.1.1 / 8.8.8.8
authoritative serverowns zone data. answers only for its zone, sets the aa flag, never asks anyone anything.root operators, TLD registries, your DNS host

the version of this post I'm replacing got the emphasis wrong here: it walked a dig +trace, in which dig itself plays recursive resolver. that's a great flashlight and a misleading default mental model — in every normal lookup your machine sends exactly one question and the walking happens elsewhere, at a resolver shared with thousands of other clients. that sharing is not a detail. it's the load-bearing wall, and the second visual below is about nothing else.

three things the animation is asserting, in prose form so they're checkable:

  • a referral is an answer with an empty ANSWER section. the root's response puts 4 NS records in AUTHORITY and their addresses in ADDITIONAL, and sets no aa on the question you asked — it is authoritative for the delegation, not the name. verified: the TLD's referral today came back ANSWER: 0, AUTHORITY: 2, flags qr only.
  • the resolver walks; nobody else does. the stub set rd=1 (recursion desired) and got back ra (recursion available). the resolver's own upstream queries set rd=0 — asking root to recurse would be both useless and rude, and root ignores the bit anyway.
  • the walk deposits three cache entries, not one. the A record is the headline, but the two referrals get cached too — at 172800 s and 900 s. tomorrow, a lookup of any .online name starts at the TLD, not at root. this is why root servers survive at all.

The part that makes it survive: caching, and the TTL

do the arithmetic the tree forces. every uncached lookup lands on the root; there are on the order of 1011 DNS lookups happening globally per hour, and 13 root server names (hundreds of anycast instances, but still). if even one lookup in a thousand touched root, the design collapses. the reason it doesn't: every answer carries a TTL — a number of seconds any cache may reuse it — and nearly every query dies in a cache before reaching anyone authoritative.

how much does a cache layer actually absorb? you can derive it. queries for one name arrive at one cache as (roughly) a Poisson stream of rate r; the cache holds the answer for T seconds. life at that cache is a cycle: one miss fills it, T seconds of hits, then it expires and the next arrival — on average 1/r seconds later — misses again. one miss per cycle of length T + 1/r, so:

miss rate  =  r / (1 + r·T)      → r when r·T ≪ 1   (cache useless: entry dies unused)
                                → 1/T when r·T ≫ 1  (load-independent: the magic)

that second limit is the whole story of DNS scale: past saturation, a cache's upstream traffic stops depending on demand entirely — it's one query per TTL per name, no matter whether a hundred or a million clients sit behind it. the simulator below runs this live with two cache layers, exactly like the real path: 1,000 client machines with their own stub caches sharing one recursive resolver. drag the load slider across two orders of magnitude and watch what the authoritative server sees.

four things to actually try in it, each a claim from this post made falsifiable:

  • load doesn't reach upstream. 50/s → 5,000/s: the orange line holds near N/TTL. the cache converts demand growth into hit-ratio growth.
  • TTL is the real throttle. drop TTL from 30 s to 2 s at fixed load — upstream traffic multiplies by ~15×. this is why "just lower the TTL" is a cost decision someone else pays for.
  • low per-name traffic makes caches useless. push names up to 500 at low load: r·T per name falls below 1, stub hit rate collapses toward 0%, and upstream ≈ demand. a cache only helps names that are actually hot — the long tail walks every time.
  • flushing the resolver is a non-event; a cold start is not. "flush the resolver's cache" barely bumps the burst — 1,000 stub caches are still absorbing. "restart everything" spikes the orange line hard. layered caches fail gracefully layer by layer, and the burst you fear needs all of them cold at once.

TTLs decrement — the number you see is the remainder

a record leaves its authoritative server with its full TTL and ages in every cache it sits in. downstream caches inherit the remainder, not the original — so expiry stays roughly synchronized across the chain instead of stacking. you can see this from here: the authoritative server hands out my A record with TTL 86400, but querying through the local stub today returned TTL 21600 — the remaining lifetime of the copy some upstream cache fetched hours ago.

this is also why "DNS propagation" is a myth in the literal sense. nothing propagates. there is no push channel, no invalidation protocol — a change becomes visible cache by cache, as each one's remainder hits zero. the practical trick falls straight out of the model: drop the TTL to 60 s one full old TTL before a migration, wait for the long-lived copies to age out everywhere, flip the record, then raise it back. you're not speeding anything up — you're pre-paying the staleness window at a moment it doesn't hurt.

absence is cached too. ask for a name that doesn't exist and the authoritative server returns NXDOMAIN plus its zone's SOA record, whose last field caps how long resolvers may remember the negative answer — measured today: 600 s for this zone (RFC 2308 is the spec). typo a hostname in a config, fix it, and still get NXDOMAIN for ten minutes: this is who did it.

What's actually on the wire

everything above rides in single UDP datagrams to port 53 — one out, one back, no connection. that choice is load-bearing: a resolver mid-walk holds no sockets, no handshakes, and a root server can answer from pure receive-loop. the packet is small enough to internalize completely:

header — 12 bytes
  ID 16-bit random    match answers to questions — and the first anti-spoofing token
  flags: qr query/response · rd recursion desired · ra recursion available
         aa authoritative · tc truncated — retry over TCP
         rcode 0 NOERROR · 2 SERVFAIL · 3 NXDOMAIN
  counts: QD 1 · AN · NS · AR       (question / answer / authority / additional)
question
  QNAME 7'mdnihal'6'online'0       length-prefixed labels, no dots on the wire
  QTYPE A (1) · QCLASS IN (1)
each resource record
  name · type · class · TTL (32-bit seconds) · rdlength · rdata

classic DNS capped UDP payloads at 512 bytes. modern responses (DNSSEC signatures, many records) don't fit, so EDNS(0) added an OPT pseudo-record advertising a bigger buffer — today's responses here advertised 1232 bytes, the post-2020 "flag day" consensus value chosen to dodge IP fragmentation. bigger than that and the server sets tc=1, and the client retries the same question over TCP.

that 16-bit ID plus the UDP source port are also the only things binding an answer to a question — an off-path attacker who guesses both first gets to be your resolver's upstream and poison a cache entry for its full TTL. 16 bits alone is race-able (the 2008 Kaminsky attack industrialized this); randomizing the source port adds another ~16 bits, and DNSSEC exists because 32 guessable bits is still not a signature. mechanism per the RFCs and the published attack write-ups; I haven't reproduced a poisoning race myself.

Where the signatures stop

DNSSEC answers one narrow question the packet format can't: did this answer actually come from the zone owner? each zone signs its records; each parent vouches for its child's key with a DS record. what makes the design honest is how it handles absence: a parent can't just omit the DS — silence is indistinguishable from an attacker stripping it — so it returns signed NSEC3 records proving no DS exists. absence gets a proof, not an omission:

root (a.root-servers.net) │ DS + RRSIG, alg 8 → vouches for .online's key, signed ▼ .online TLD (ns01.trs-dns.net) │ NSEC3 + RRSIG, alg 13 → proves NO DS exists for mdnihal.online ▼ mdnihal.online (ns2.dns-parking.com, fronted by Cloudflare) │ A record, unsigned → taken on faith from here down ▼ 216.198.79.1 (Vercel)

so this domain's chain of trust is exactly one hop long, and provably so — .online signed a statement that mdnihal.online opted out. at every hop of any trace, look for which of DS or NSEC3 appears: that single check locates the end of any domain's chain of trust. RDAP-verified along the way: the nameserver answers from Cloudflare address space, the A record is Vercel's — two providers a plain dig would flatten into one line.

symptommeaningwhere to look

SERVFAILthe resolver tried and failed: unreachable authoritatives, a broken delegation, or a DNSSEC validation failure. says nothing about existence.dig +cd (skip validation) and dig @auth directly to split the cases

the general debugging move is the same one the whole post has been making: ask each layer directly and compare. stub (resolvectl query), resolver (dig @1.1.1.1), authoritative (dig @ns2… +norecurse). the layer where the answer changes is the layer that's lying to you. and know what you can actually flush: resolvectl flush-caches empties your stub — the resolver's cache belongs to whoever runs the resolver, and their remainder expires on their schedule, not yours.

The shape worth keeping

strip the protocol away and DNS is a read-mostly key-value store that scales by giving up on invalidation: writes are delegated to owners (the tree), reads are amortized by every intermediate holding copies (caches), and correctness is bounded-staleness with an explicit knob (the TTL) instead of coherence. lots of systems are this shape wearing different costumes — a CDN with max-age, a service-discovery layer, a feature-flag client polling on an interval. same trade every time: the owner buys read scale by promising not to change their mind faster than the lease they handed out. when you meet one, ask the DNS questions — who owns writes? what's the lease? what happens at expiry, thundering or staggered? — and you already understand it.
